All three charts now use the same y-axis unit: ounces of gold. Each line shows how many ounces were required to buy one cow, one acre of U.S. farmland, or one hour of U.S. manufacturing labor. The cow/cattle chart now spans roughly 300 years. Before 1867 it uses sparse historical benchmark observations rather than a continuous national series: colonial New England cattle prices for the 1720s–1770s, a 1790s New York cow benchmark, and an 1825 Detroit-area probate benchmark. From 1867 onward the series shifts to U.S. milk/replacement-cow data. The farmland series reaches back to the 19th century, while the consistent manufacturing-wage comparison is primarily a 20th- and 21st-century series.
300-year cow-series methodology: Early colonial points use decadal New England cattle-price observations converted from sterling into fine-gold ounces using the British post-1717 mint equivalent. They are directional purchasing-power benchmarks, not a seamless continuation of the later USDA milk-cow series.

Why these anchors?

What each series captures and how long the relationship has been observable

Productive cows · historical evidence spanning centuries

Gold vs. productive livestock

Cattle are a useful real-economy benchmark because they are productive assets with biological scarcity, labor inputs, and direct economic utility. Historical references from ancient and medieval economies sometimes valued a cow at roughly a fraction of an ounce to about one ounce of gold, while modern U.S. replacement-cow values have at times returned close to the one-ounce range. The relationship is loose rather than fixed, but it has persisted as an order-of-magnitude purchasing-power comparison for a very long time.

U.S. farmland · national data from the 19th century onward

Gold vs. scarce land

Farmland is one of the cleanest long-duration scarce-asset comparisons. U.S. national farm-real-estate data extend back into the 1800s. Gold's purchasing power against farmland fell sharply while gold was constrained near $35 per ounce in the mid-20th century, then rebounded after the gold window closed. This makes farmland especially useful for testing whether monetary regimes created valuation gaps between gold and freely repricing real assets.

Manufacturing wages · consistent U.S. series from the 20th century

Gold vs. labor

Labor provides a real-economy price benchmark rather than a pure scarce asset. Gold bought about 24 hours of manufacturing labor in 1950, roughly 11 hours in 1970, and far more after gold was allowed to float. The relationship is informative, but productivity, technology, unions, globalization, and labor-market structure can shift wages independently of money, so labor is treated as a diagnostic rather than a direct fair-value anchor.

Important: these series do not move one-for-one with gold. The model looks for long-run purchasing-power relationships, not short-run price correlation. Gold can diverge from cows, land, and wages for years or decades because interest rates, monetary policy, investor demand, productivity, and government intervention affect each series differently. The most useful signal is the size and persistence of the valuation gap.

Fair value

Land fair value = Land $/acre × Acres-per-oz equilibrium
Core fair value = √(Cow FV × Land FV)
Monetary premium
Premium = (Gold spot ÷ Core fair value) − 1
Scenario price = Future fair value × (1 + Scenario premium)
