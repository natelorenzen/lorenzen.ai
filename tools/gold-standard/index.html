Gold Standard · purchasing-power model

What is an ounce of gold actually worth?

Over the long run, gold's price has loosely tracked what it costs to buy a productive cow or an acre of U.S. farmland. This model prices gold against those real assets and measures the monetary premium investors pay on top.

Method and sources

Fair value

index = √(cow $/head × land $/acre × 0.6)
fair_value = ea × indexb
a, b = least squares on ln(gold) vs ln(index), 1972–2025

Premium and scenarios

premium = gold ÷ fair_value − 1
fair_12m = fair_value × (1 + g₁₀)
price_12m = fair_12m × (1 + premium_12m)
reversion: ln(1+p)₁₂ = ln(1+p) × (1 + β)

g₁₀ is the trailing 10-year growth rate of fair value. β is the slope from regressing each year's change in log premium on the prior year's level (1972–2025).

Data

  1. Gold: annual U.S. dollar price per troy ounce; official/fixed price before 1968, London market thereafter.
  2. Cows: USDA NASS price of milk/replacement cows, $/head, with USDA ERS annual dairy-cow prices filling 1981–2019; remaining gaps (mostly 1938–1979) linearly interpolated.
  3. Inflation: BLS CPI-U, U.S. city average, annual average (FRED CPIAUCNS). 2026 is estimated by carrying forward 2025's inflation rate.
  4. Farmland: USDA farm real-estate value, $/acre (Census of Agriculture and Land Values surveys).
  5. Wages: BLS average hourly earnings, manufacturing production workers (CES3000000008).
  6. 2026 reference values: gold $3,628 (Nov 19, 2026), cow $3,160 (Oct 2026), farmland $4,500 (2026). Provisional.

Why cows and farmland?

Both are productive, scarce, real assets that reprice freely and have long U.S. price records. Ancient and medieval sources often value a cow somewhere between a fraction of an ounce and about an ounce of gold, and modern U.S. replacement cows have returned to roughly that range. Farmland offers the cleanest long-run scarce-asset comparison: gold's purchasing power over land collapsed while gold was pinned at $35, then recovered after the float.

How is fair value calibrated?

The model originally used fixed anchors: one cow or 0.6 acres per ounce. Against those, gold traded about 45% below "fair value" in a typical floating-era year. Shifting the line down fixed the average but left today's fair value near $1,600, because gold has outgrown cows and farmland over time. So fair value now has a fitted slope as well as a level: since 1972, gold has risen about 1.5% for every 1% rise in the cow-and-farmland index. That fits better (log R² 0.84 vs. 0.75) and, fitted on 1972–2000 alone, tracked 2001–2025 more closely than the level-only version. It also means "cows per ounce" drifts upward over time instead of staying fixed. A slope is one more fitted number, so treat it as a description of history. The CSV keeps the original anchors in modeled_gold_usd_oz; the page fits the slope on top. Set the slope slider to 1.00 to see the level-only version.

Fair value now uses a fitted level and slope against the cow-and-farmland index (1972–2025), with an out-of-sample check. Connected live gold and wage data. Replaced 36 interpolated cow-price years (1981–2019) with USDA ERS annual dairy-cow prices. Added an inflation-only (CPI) baseline to the chart and a head-to-head test against it.
