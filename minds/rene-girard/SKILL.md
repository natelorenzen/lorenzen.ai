# Purpose

This skill asks whose desire is being imitated. It looks for rivals converging because they watch each other, conflicts that escalate through imitation, and groups that resolve tension by blaming a scapegoat instead of addressing causes. Girard developed his theory from literature, anthropology, and religion. Applying it to markets and organizations is an extension, labeled OPERATIONALIZED. The skill does not simulate or speak for René Girard.

# When to Use

- Competitors converging on the same features, positioning, or strategy
- Desire for a deal, market, or hire that seems driven by who else wants it
- Escalating internal conflicts or feuds between rivals
- Post-failure blame that settles on one person or group

Do not use for:

- Problems with clear material or economic explanations, except as a secondary check

# Core Questions

- **Whose desire is being imitated?** (MINDS summary, not a quotation)
- Are rivals converging because they watch each other?
- Is conflict escalating through imitation?
- Who is being made the scapegoat?

# Documented Principles

| ID | Principle (MINDS' wording) | Provenance | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| P1 | Desire is mimetic: people come to desire objects because a model or "mediator" desires them, not only for the objects' intrinsic value. | DOCUMENTED | S1; S4 |
| P2 | In external mediation the model is distant, so there is no rivalry. In internal mediation the model is close, a peer, and the model becomes a rival for the same object. | DOCUMENTED | S1; S4 |
| P3 | Mimetic rivalry escalates. Rivals imitate each other's desires and hostility, and focus shifts from the object to the rival. | DOCUMENTED | S2, S3; S4 |
| P4 | Communities discharge accumulated mimetic tension by uniting against a scapegoat, which restores order without resolving the cause. | DOCUMENTED | S2, S3; S4 |
| P5 | Competitive convergence in markets, such as feature races and me-too strategies, can be read as internal mediation between firms. | OPERATIONALIZED | P2, P3 |
| P6 | The agent must identify the "model" whose desire the decision-maker may be imitating, and test whether the desire survives without that model. | OPERATIONALIZED | P1 |

# Analytical Procedure

1. **Models (P1, P6).** Who does the decision-maker, team, or market look to? Would the object still be wanted if the model didn't want it?
2. **Distance (P2).** Is the model distant (inspiration) or a close peer (rivalry)?
3. **Escalation (P3, P5).** Are competitors mirroring each other? Has the focus shifted from customers to beating the rival?
4. **Scapegoat check (P4).** After failures or in conflict, is blame converging on a person, team, or outsider in a way that avoids the systemic cause?
5. **Conclude.** Name the mimetic dynamic, if any, and how to step out of it, for example by differentiating or by re-anchoring on the customer.

# What This Framework Pays Attention To

- Imitation, rivalry, and convergence
- Escalation dynamics
- Scapegoating as a false resolution

# What This Framework May Underweight

- Material, economic, and structural explanations (see Porter, Deming)
- Its own analogy limits outside its original domains

# Questions to Ask

- Would we want this if our rival didn't?
- Are we competing for customers or against a competitor?
- What have we started copying from the people we most dislike?
- Is the person being blamed really the cause?
