# Purpose

This skill asks who will be the lowest-cost producer at scale, and which parts of the value chain should be owned or combined to get there. This is a framework derived from Rockefeller's memoir, read alongside the historical record. It does not simulate or speak for John D. Rockefeller, and it does not endorse Standard Oil's documented anticompetitive practices.

# When to Use

- Cost structure and operating efficiency
- Consolidation, roll-ups, and mergers in fragmented industries
- Vertical integration decisions (make versus buy, owning logistics)
- Commodity businesses where cost is the advantage

Do not use for:

- Differentiated or brand-led businesses, except as a cost check
- Any recommendation that would breach competition law

# Core Questions

- **Who will be the lowest-cost producer at scale?** (MINDS summary, not a quotation)
- Where is waste being thrown away that could become value?
- Which parts of the chain should we own?
- What does combination make possible that separate firms cannot do?

# Documented Principles

| ID | Principle (MINDS' wording) | Provenance | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| P1 | Improve processes so the product can be made and sold cheaply yet at a profit, and turn into by-products the materials that less efficient competitors discard. | DOCUMENTED | S1 |
| P2 | Combining separate firms' skill and capital can achieve economy and efficiency that the smaller businesses could not reach alone. | DOCUMENTED | S1 |
| P3 | Integrate into the functions the core business depends on, such as barrels, tanks, pipelines, and transport, when outside supply is costly or unreliable. | DOCUMENTED | S1 |
| P4 | Before entering an enterprise, make sure you can see your way to staying through to a successful end. Look ahead, and avoid petty or temporary advantages. | DOCUMENTED | S1 |
| P5 | Relentless measurement of costs, down to small units, was central to Standard Oil's advantage. | DERIVED | S2 |
| P6 | Scale advantages invite legal and political backlash. Standard Oil was broken up under antitrust law in 1911. | DOCUMENTED (historical record) | S2 |
| P7 | The agent must pair every consolidation or integration recommendation with its regulatory and legitimacy risk. | OPERATIONALIZED | P6 |

# Analytical Procedure

1. **Cost map (P1, P5).** Break the unit cost into components. Where is the business above best-in-class, and where is value being discarded?
2. **Scale (P2).** Would combination or greater volume lower unit costs materially? Show the mechanism, not just the size.
3. **Integration (P3).** Which inputs or channels are costly, unreliable, or controlled by others? Weigh make versus buy.
4. **Staying power (P4).** Can the firm see its way through to the end of the strategy, including financing through downturns?
5. **Backlash (P6, P7).** Assess antitrust, regulatory, and reputational exposure.
6. **Conclude.** Describe the cost-advantage path and its limits.

# What This Framework Pays Attention To

- Unit costs, waste, and efficiency
- Scale and consolidation economics
- Control of critical inputs and logistics

# What This Framework May Underweight

- Customer experience and brand (see Ogilvy, Bezos)
- Legitimacy and ethics, beyond the explicit check in P7
- Innovation that makes the cost base obsolete (see Christensen)

# Questions to Ask

- What is our unit cost versus the best competitor's, component by component?
- What are we throwing away that someone would pay for?
- Which dependency could a supplier or channel use against us?
- How would regulators and the public view this if it succeeds?
