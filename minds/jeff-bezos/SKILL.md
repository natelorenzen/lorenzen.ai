# Purpose

This skill examines a decision for its reversibility and time horizon. It asks whether the decision is a one-way or two-way door, whether the organization is moving at the right speed, whether it is optimizing for the long term or for appearances, and whether it has substituted proxies for customers. This is a framework derived from Bezos's shareholder letters. It does not simulate or speak for Jeff Bezos.

# When to Use

- Deciding how much analysis a decision deserves
- Long-term investment versus short-term profit trade-offs
- Organizations that have become slow, process-bound, or inward-looking
- Experiments, new product bets, and tolerance for failure

Do not use for:

- Organizations without access to patient capital, unless the constraint is made explicit

# Core Questions

- **Is this a one-way door or a two-way door?** (MINDS summary, not a quotation)
- Are we deciding at the speed this decision deserves?
- Are we optimizing long-term cash flow or short-term appearances?
- Are we managing to a proxy instead of to the customer?
- Is this an experiment we can afford to fail?

# Documented Principles

| ID | Principle (MINDS' wording) | Provenance | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| P1 | Separate irreversible, consequential "Type 1" decisions (one-way doors), which deserve slow, careful deliberation, from reversible "Type 2" decisions (two-way doors), which should be made quickly by small groups. Large organizations tend to apply heavy Type 1 process to everything. | DOCUMENTED | S2 |
| P2 | Most decisions should be made with roughly 70% of the information you would like. Waiting for 90% is usually too slow, provided you are good at recognizing and correcting bad decisions. | DOCUMENTED | S3 |
| P3 | "Disagree and commit": when there is conviction but no consensus, ask for commitment rather than prolonged debate. | DOCUMENTED | S3 |
| P4 | "Day 2" (stasis) is resisted through customer obsession, skepticism of proxies (such as process or surveys standing in for the customer), eager adoption of external trends, and high-velocity decision-making. | DOCUMENTED | S3 |
| P5 | Make investment decisions for long-term market leadership rather than short-term profitability or Wall Street reaction. Prefer maximizing the present value of future cash flows over the appearance of accounting results. | DOCUMENTED | S1 |
| P6 | Failure and invention are inseparable. Experiments, by definition, may not work. | DOCUMENTED | S2 |
| P7 | The agent must classify each major choice as a one-way or two-way door before recommending a decision process. | OPERATIONALIZED | P1 |

# Analytical Procedure

1. **Classify reversibility (P1, P7).** For each option, ask whether it can be undone, at what cost, and how quickly. Separate the one-way doors from the two-way doors.
2. **Match the process to the door (P1, P2).** Two-way doors: decide quickly with about 70% of the information, and set up fast correction. One-way doors: slow down, consult, and model the downside.
3. **Time horizon (P5).** Compare the options on long-term cash flow and position, not near-term optics. Name any metric that is flattering but misleading.
4. **Proxies versus customer (P4).** Check whether the decision is driven by a process, a metric, or a survey that has replaced direct understanding of the customer.
5. **External trends (P4).** Ask whether a major external trend is being resisted rather than adopted.
6. **Experiment design (P6).** Where uncertainty is high, frame the next step as an affordable experiment with a clear readout.
7. **Deadlock (P3).** If the team is stuck, recommend disagree-and-commit with an explicit review point.

# What This Framework Pays Attention To

- Reversibility and the cost of delay
- Long-term value versus appearance
- Customer needs over internal proxies
- Organizational speed and drift

# What This Framework May Underweight

- Firms that must show near-term profits to survive
- Tail risks hidden inside decisions that look reversible (see Taleb)
- Competitive structure (see Porter)

# Questions to Ask

- If we get this wrong, can we walk back through the door, and at what cost?
- What would we decide today with the information we have?
- Which metric are we optimizing that the customer doesn't care about?
- What external trend are we treating as a threat instead of a tailwind?
- What is the cheapest experiment that would teach us the most?
