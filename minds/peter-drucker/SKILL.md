# Purpose

This skill asks the foundational management questions: what the business is, who the customer is, what the customer values, and whether leadership's time and the organization's resources are going to the results that matter. This is a framework derived from Drucker's books. It does not simulate or speak for Peter Drucker.

# When to Use

- An organization unclear about its purpose, customer, or priorities
- Executive focus: what to do, what to stop doing
- Setting objectives and accountability
- Finding sources of innovation

Do not use for:

- Pure financial valuation or competitive tactics

# Core Questions

- **What is our business, and what does the customer value?** (MINDS summary, not a quotation)
- What should we stop doing?
- What results are we accountable for?
- Where are the opportunities for innovation?

# Documented Principles

| ID | Principle (MINDS' wording) | Provenance | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| P1 | The purpose of a business is to create a customer. Marketing and innovation are therefore its two basic functions. | DOCUMENTED | S1 |
| P2 | Management must ask explicitly: what is our business, who is the customer, what does the customer value, and what should our business be? The answers come from the customer's point of view. | DOCUMENTED | S1 |
| P3 | Management by objectives and self-control: objectives derived from the needs of the business, with people measuring their own performance against them. | DOCUMENTED | S1 |
| P4 | Executive effectiveness can be learned: know where your time goes, focus on contribution, build on strengths, concentrate on first things, and make effective decisions. | DOCUMENTED | S2 |
| P5 | Innovation can be systematic. There are seven sources: the unexpected, incongruities, process need, changes in industry or market structure, demographics, changes in perception, and new knowledge. | DOCUMENTED | S3 |
| P6 | Organizations should systematically abandon activities, products, and policies that no longer contribute, to free resources for opportunities. | DERIVED | S2, S3 |
| P7 | The agent must restate the problem in terms of the customer and the results expected before recommending any internal change. | OPERATIONALIZED | P1, P2 |

# Analytical Procedure

1. **Define the business (P1, P2, P7).** State who the customer is, what they value, and what the business should be. Note any disagreement inside the organization.
2. **Results (P3).** State the results that matter, and whether objectives and measures point at them.
3. **Contribution and focus (P4).** Where does leadership time actually go? Is it concentrated on the few things that matter most?
4. **Abandonment (P6).** What should be stopped to free resources?
5. **Innovation sources (P5).** Scan the seven sources for opportunity, especially the unexpected success or failure.
6. **Conclude.** Name the priorities, the stop list, and the accountability.

# What This Framework Pays Attention To

- Purpose, customer, and results
- Effectiveness over efficiency
- Systematic abandonment and innovation

# What This Framework May Underweight

- Competitive tactics and industry structure (see Porter)
- Financial valuation (see Buffett)
- Fast adversarial dynamics (see Boyd)

# Questions to Ask

- If we weren't already doing this, would we start it today?
- Who is the customer who matters most, and what do they value?
- What unexpected success are we ignoring?
- What result is each person accountable for?
