# Purpose

This skill asks what the environment is selecting for, and which variations survive. It treats markets, products, and organizations as populations that change through variation and selection. It also borrows Darwin's documented discipline of recording every fact that contradicts his own theory. Applying natural selection to business is an analogy, labeled OPERATIONALIZED. This is not Darwin's claim, and the skill does not simulate or speak for him.

# When to Use

- Competitive dynamics where many players try many things and the environment decides
- Markets whose conditions are shifting, with the question of which strategies will survive
- Deciding between planning one solution and generating many variations and selecting
- Long-horizon evidence-gathering on a contested question

Do not use for:

- Single deliberate decisions with no population or selection dynamic, except as a wildcard lens

# Core Questions

- **What is the environment selecting for?** (MINDS summary, not a quotation)
- Which variations survive, and why?
- What changed in the environment?
- Which facts do not fit our theory?

# Documented Principles

| ID | Principle (MINDS' wording) | Provenance | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| P1 | Individuals vary. Favorable variations tend to be preserved and unfavorable ones destroyed under the struggle for existence, which accumulates into new forms. | DOCUMENTED | S1, S2 |
| P2 | Change by natural selection is gradual, accumulating small advantageous variations over long periods. | DOCUMENTED | S1 |
| P3 | Darwin kept a "golden rule": whenever he met a fact or thought opposed to his general results, he wrote it down at once, because such facts were more likely to be forgotten than favorable ones. | DOCUMENTED | S2 |
| P4 | Treat competitors, products, and strategies as a population. What survives depends on fit with the current environment, not on intrinsic quality or intent. | OPERATIONALIZED | P1 |
| P5 | Where the environment is uncertain, generating many cheap variations and letting outcomes select can beat a single planned design. | OPERATIONALIZED | P1, P2 |
| P6 | The agent must keep an explicit list of disconfirming facts alongside the analysis. | OPERATIONALIZED | P3 |

# Analytical Procedure

1. **Environment (P4).** Describe the selective environment: customers, costs, regulation, and technology. What has changed recently?
2. **Population and variation (P1, P4).** Which variants exist among competitors, business models, or products? Which are gaining share, and what trait explains it?
3. **Fit (P4).** Is our variant fitted to the environment as it now is, or as it was?
4. **Variation strategy (P5).** Should we plan one solution, or run several cheap variants and select?
5. **Disconfirming evidence (P3, P6).** List the facts that don't fit the leading explanation.
6. **Conclude.** State what the environment rewards, and how to adapt.

# What This Framework Pays Attention To

- Selection pressures and shifting environments
- Variation and survival
- Evidence that contradicts the preferred story

# What This Framework May Underweight

- Deliberate strategic intent and foresight (see Porter, Thiel)
- Short time horizons, since selection is slow
- The limits of the analogy: firms can plan and learn, and organisms cannot

# Questions to Ask

- What trait do the survivors in this market share?
- What has changed in the environment that our strategy was fitted to?
- Could we run five cheap experiments instead of one expensive bet?
- What facts have we been quietly forgetting?
