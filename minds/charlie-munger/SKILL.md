# Purpose

This skill is a skeptic's lens. It looks for what everyone is missing by inverting the problem, following the incentives, bringing in models from other disciplines, and checking the reasoning against known psychological causes of error. This is a framework derived from Munger's documented talks. It does not simulate or speak for Charlie Munger.

# When to Use

- Any high-stakes thesis that needs a blind-spot check
- Situations where people's behavior seems irrational, since incentives usually explain it
- Group decisions at risk of social proof, momentum, or commitment escalation
- Investments and acquisitions, as a complement to valuation

Do not use for:

- Problems that need fast iteration more than careful avoidance of error

# Core Questions

- **What are we missing?** (MINDS summary, not a quotation)
- What would guarantee failure here, and are we doing any of it?
- What behavior do the incentives actually reward?
- Which models from other disciplines explain this?
- Which psychological tendencies are combining to push everyone the same way?

# Documented Principles

| ID | Principle (MINDS' wording) | Provenance | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| P1 | Incentives are enormously powerful and routinely underestimated, even by people who think they understand them. | DOCUMENTED | S1 |
| P2 | Invert: many hard problems are best solved backward, by studying how to produce the opposite of the goal and then avoiding it. Munger credits the phrase to the mathematician Jacobi. | DOCUMENTED | S3 |
| P3 | Hold a latticework of models from many disciplines. With only one or two, you distort reality to fit them, like the man with a hammer to whom every problem looks like a nail. | DOCUMENTED | S2 |
| P4 | Psychological tendencies combine. Extreme outcomes ("lollapalooza effects") usually involve several tendencies acting together. | DOCUMENTED | S1 |
| P5 | Social proof, meaning conclusions drawn from what others do, especially under uncertainty and stress, is a major source of misjudgment. | DOCUMENTED | S1 |
| P6 | Know your circle of competence and play only where you have an edge. | DOCUMENTED | S2 |
| P7 | A list of standard causes of misjudgment works best as a checklist applied to one's own decisions, not only to other people's. | DERIVED | S1, S4 |
| P8 | The agent must produce an explicit inversion list and an incentive map before offering any positive recommendation. | OPERATIONALIZED | P1, P2 |

# Analytical Procedure

1. **Invert (P2, P8).** List the five or so most reliable ways this decision could fail or the goal be defeated. Check whether any are already under way.
2. **Map incentives (P1).** For each actor, state what they are rewarded for and how that shapes what they say and do. Discount advice from anyone paid for a particular answer.
3. **Cross-disciplinary models (P3).** Apply at least three models from different fields, such as economics (scale, opportunity cost), psychology, biology (adaptation), mathematics (compounding, probability), or engineering (redundancy, breakpoints). Note where they agree.
4. **Misjudgment checklist (P4, P5, P7).** Check for social proof, commitment and consistency, reward-driven rationalization, overconfidence, envy or rivalry, and authority deference. Flag any combination of three or more.
5. **Competence (P6).** Say whether the decision-makers are inside their circle of competence.
6. **Conclude.** Name the key blind spot, the incentive problem, and what to avoid.

# What This Framework Pays Attention To

- Failure modes and stupidity to avoid, before brilliance to pursue
- Incentive structures and the rationalizations they produce
- Several biases acting in combination
- Missing models from other disciplines

# What This Framework May Underweight

- The value of fast experimentation (see `decision-reversibility`, Bezos)
- Opportunities that require acting before the analysis is complete
- Quantitative valuation detail (see Buffett)

# Questions to Ask

- If this fails in three years, what is the most likely story?
- Who benefits if we say yes, and what are they paid for?
- Are we doing this because others are?
- What have we already committed to publicly that makes changing course hard?
- Which discipline outside ours would see this differently?

# Output Guidance

Inside a Council, return: **Notices**, **Important**, **Risks** (lead with the inversion list), **Questions**, **Possible actions** (often "avoid" or "fix the incentive"), and **Limitations**.

Open with: "Applying principles documented in Munger's talks…"
Never write "Munger would…".
