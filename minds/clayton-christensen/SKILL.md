# Purpose

This skill asks whether the basis of competition is changing, and why well-run incumbents fail to respond. It also asks what "job" customers are really hiring the product to do. This is a framework derived from Christensen's books. It does not simulate or speak for Clayton Christensen.

# When to Use

- A cheaper, simpler entrant is gaining ground at the low end or in a new market
- Deciding whether and how an incumbent should respond to a new technology or model
- Innovation strategy and new market entry
- Understanding why customers choose, switch, or don't buy

Do not use for:

- Every competitive threat. Many are not disruptive, and misapplying the label is a known failure mode.

# Core Questions

- **What changes when the basis of competition changes?** (MINDS summary, not a quotation)
- Are we overshooting what mainstream customers can use?
- Is the entrant coming in at the low end, or creating a new market of non-consumers?
- What job is the customer hiring this product to do?

# Documented Principles

| ID | Principle (MINDS' wording) | Provenance | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| P1 | Sustaining innovations improve performance along dimensions existing customers value. Incumbents usually win these. Disruptive innovations start worse on those dimensions but cheaper, simpler, or more accessible. | DOCUMENTED | S1 |
| P2 | Well-managed incumbents fail against disruption precisely because they listen to their best customers and pursue higher margins. Good management causes the failure. | DOCUMENTED | S1 |
| P3 | Performance improvement often outpaces what mainstream customers can use. That overshoot opens room for "good enough" entrants. | DOCUMENTED | S1 |
| P4 | There are two footholds for disruption: low-end (overserved customers) and new-market (non-consumers). | DOCUMENTED | S2 |
| P5 | An organization's capabilities sit in its resources, processes, and values. Processes and values that fit the core business disable it for disruptive work. | DOCUMENTED | S1, S2 |
| P6 | Pursue a disruptive opportunity through an autonomous organization whose cost structure and values fit the small, new market. | DOCUMENTED | S1 |
| P7 | Customers "hire" products to make progress in a particular circumstance. Understanding the job explains demand better than customer attributes do. | DOCUMENTED | S3 |
| P8 | The agent must first test whether a threat fits the disruption pattern (P1, P4) and say explicitly if it does not. | OPERATIONALIZED | P1, P4 |

# Analytical Procedure

1. **Pattern test (P1, P4, P8).** Is the threat a better product for the best customers (sustaining), or a cheaper, simpler product for overserved customers or non-consumers (disruptive)? If neither, say that disruption theory has limited purchase here.
2. **Overshoot (P3).** Where are mainstream customers paying for performance they don't use?
3. **Incumbent logic (P2, P5).** Explain why the incumbent's margins, customers, and processes make responding unattractive. This is where the risk lies.
4. **Jobs (P7).** State the job customers are hiring for, including its functional, emotional, and social dimensions. Who else competes for that job?
5. **Response design (P6).** If a response is warranted, consider a separate unit with its own cost structure and metrics.
6. **Conclude.** Describe the trajectory of the threat and the window to act.

# What This Framework Pays Attention To

- Low-end and non-consumption footholds
- Margins and processes that blind incumbents
- The job behind the purchase

# What This Framework May Underweight

- Network effects and platform dynamics
- Threats that start at the high end
- Financial valuation (see Buffett)

# Questions to Ask

- Which of our customers would be happy with less, for less?
- Who doesn't buy at all today, and why?
- What would our own processes kill if we tried this internally?
- What job does the customer actually need done?
