# Purpose

This skill asks whether we actually know what we think we know. It questions the key terms in an argument, looks for contradictions, and treats the discovery of ignorance as progress. Socrates wrote nothing. This framework is DERIVED from Plato's dialogues, which are the main but not neutral source. It does not simulate or speak for Socrates.

# When to Use

- A strategy or debate built on undefined terms such as "quality", "customer-centric", "win", or "AI-first"
- Teams that are confident but have never examined their premises
- Evaluating an argument for internal contradictions
- Getting unstuck by admitting what isn't known

Do not use for:

- Situations where a decision is needed now and the definitions are good enough

# Core Questions

- **Do we actually know what we think we know?** (MINDS summary, not a quotation)
- What do we mean by this term?
- What follows if this is true?
- Where does our reasoning contradict itself?

# Documented Principles

All principles are drawn from Plato's portrayal of Socrates.

| ID | Principle (MINDS' wording) | Provenance | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| P1 | Recognizing one's own ignorance is a form of wisdom. Socrates holds himself wiser only in not thinking he knows what he doesn't. | DOCUMENTED (in Plato) | S1 |
| P2 | The unexamined life is not worth living. Continual examination of oneself and others is the greatest good. | DOCUMENTED (in Plato) | S1 |
| P3 | Ask for a definition of the essence ("What is virtue?"), not a list of examples. | DOCUMENTED (in Plato) | S2 |
| P4 | Perplexity (aporia) is productive. Realizing you don't know is the precondition for inquiry. | DOCUMENTED (in Plato) | S2 |
| P5 | The agent must extract the three to five load-bearing terms in the problem statement and ask for a definition of each before analysis. | OPERATIONALIZED | P3 |

# Analytical Procedure

1. **Key terms (P3, P5).** Identify the load-bearing words in the problem and ask for precise definitions, not examples.
2. **Test the definitions.** Offer a counterexample to each. Does the definition survive?
3. **Follow implications.** If the claim is true, what else must be true? Is that acceptable?
4. **Find contradictions.** Where do two held beliefs conflict?
5. **Admit ignorance (P1, P4).** State plainly what we don't know, and what inquiry would address it.

# What This Framework Pays Attention To

- Definitions and conceptual clarity
- Hidden contradictions
- Honest acknowledgment of ignorance

# What This Framework May Underweight

- Reaching a timely decision
- Empirical data (see Bacon, Feynman)

# Questions to Ask

- What exactly do we mean by the words our strategy depends on?
- Can we name a case that fits our definition but that we'd reject?
- What would have to be true for us to be right, and do we believe all of it?
- What don't we know that we're acting as if we do?

# Output Guidance

Inside a Council, return: **Notices**, **Important**, **Risks**, **Questions** (usually the main output), **Possible actions**, and **Limitations**.
