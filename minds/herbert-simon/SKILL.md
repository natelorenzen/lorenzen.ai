# Purpose

This skill asks what decision-makers can realistically know, process, and attend to, and how the organization and its environment shape the choices they make. It favors good-enough procedures that fit real limits over idealized optimization. This is a framework derived from Simon's published work. It does not simulate or speak for Herbert Simon.

# When to Use

- Organizations overwhelmed with information or options
- Designing decision processes, rules of thumb, and division of labor
- Explaining why an organization makes "irrational" choices
- Deciding whether to keep searching or accept a good-enough option

Do not use for:

- Adversarial strategy (see Sun Tzu, Boyd)

# Core Questions

- **What can the decision-makers realistically know and process?** (MINDS summary, not a quotation)
- Is "good enough" the right standard here, and what is the aspiration level?
- Where is attention the scarce resource?
- How does the organization's structure shape what gets decided?

# Documented Principles

| ID | Principle (MINDS' wording) | Provenance | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| P1 | Rationality is bounded by limited information, limited computation, and limited time. Real decision-makers do not maximize. | DOCUMENTED | S1, S2 |
| P2 | Organisms and people adapt well enough to "satisfice" rather than optimize. They search until an option meets an aspiration level. | DOCUMENTED | S2 |
| P3 | The structure of the environment, not only the decision-maker's mind, determines which simplifications work well. | DOCUMENTED | S2 |
| P4 | Organizations shape individual decisions by setting premises: goals, information flows, and roles. Changing those premises changes the decisions. | DOCUMENTED | S1 |
| P5 | Complex systems are easier to design and understand when they are nearly decomposable: hierarchies of subsystems that interact weakly. | DOCUMENTED | S3 |
| P6 | In an information-rich world, attention is the scarce resource. Information consumes the attention of its recipients. | DOCUMENTED | S4 |
| P7 | The agent must state an explicit aspiration level (the good-enough criterion) and a stopping rule for search. | OPERATIONALIZED | P2 |

# Analytical Procedure

1. **Bounds (P1).** List what the decision-makers can't know or compute. Is the analysis pretending they can?
2. **Aspiration and stopping (P2, P7).** Define good enough, and when to stop searching.
3. **Environment fit (P3).** Which rules of thumb suit this environment's structure, and which will fail?
4. **Decision premises (P4).** What goals, information, and roles are producing the current decisions? Change those, not just the people.
5. **Decomposition (P5).** Can the problem or organization be split into loosely coupled parts?
6. **Attention (P6).** Where is attention being consumed? What information should be filtered out?

# What This Framework Pays Attention To

- Cognitive and informational limits
- Satisficing and stopping rules
- How organizational structure frames choice

# What This Framework May Underweight

- Competitive strategy and positioning (see Porter)
- Emotional, social, and mimetic motives (see Girard, Cialdini)

# Questions to Ask

- What would "good enough" look like, concretely?
- What information are people drowning in that nobody uses?
- What premises is the organization feeding into this decision?
- Which parts of this problem can be solved independently?
