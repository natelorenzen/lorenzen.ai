# Purpose

This skill asks whether a failure comes from a person or from the system that produces it. It separates ordinary variation from real signals, warns against blaming individuals for system outcomes, and looks for the management practices that drive fear, short-termism, and poor quality. This is a framework derived from Deming's writings. It does not simulate or speak for W. Edwards Deming.

# When to Use

- The same problem keeps recurring despite corrective action
- Performance management, targets, rankings, and incentives
- Quality, reliability, and process problems
- Leaders reacting to every fluctuation in a metric

Do not use for:

- One-off strategic choices with no repeating process

# Core Questions

- **Is the person failing, or is the system producing the failure?** (MINDS summary, not a quotation)
- Is this variation from a common cause or a special cause?
- What does the process reliably produce if nothing changes?
- Are targets and ratings creating fear or gaming?

# Documented Principles

| ID | Principle (MINDS' wording) | Provenance | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| P1 | The bulk of the causes of low quality and low productivity belong to the system, and so lie beyond the power of the workforce. Exhortations and blame create adversarial relationships. | DOCUMENTED | S3 (Point 10) |
| P2 | Distinguish common-cause variation (inherent in the system) from special-cause variation (from specific events). Treating one as the other, by tampering or ignoring, makes things worse. | DOCUMENTED | S1, S2 |
| P3 | Cease dependence on inspection to achieve quality. Build quality into the process. | DOCUMENTED | S3 (Point 3) |
| P4 | Drive out fear, so that everyone can work effectively. Break down barriers between departments. | DOCUMENTED | S3 (Points 8, 9) |
| P5 | Eliminate slogans, exhortations, and numerical targets for the workforce. Abolish annual or merit ratings and management by objective. | DOCUMENTED | S3 (Points 10–12) |
| P6 | Create constancy of purpose toward improving product and service. | DOCUMENTED | S3 (Point 1) |
| P7 | Management needs "profound knowledge": appreciation for a system, knowledge of variation, theory of knowledge, and psychology. | DOCUMENTED | S2 |
| P8 | The agent must classify the observed problem as common-cause or special-cause, with the evidence, before recommending action. | OPERATIONALIZED | P2 |

# Analytical Procedure

1. **Describe the process.** What system produces this outcome, including inputs, steps, handoffs, and management practices?
2. **Variation (P2, P8).** Is the problem within the normal range of the process (common cause) or outside it (special cause)? What does the data over time show?
3. **Person or system (P1).** Would anyone in this role, with these tools and rules, produce the same result? If so, fix the system.
4. **Management practices (P4, P5).** Are targets, ratings, or fear driving gaming, hiding, or local optimization?
5. **Inspection versus prevention (P3).** Is quality being inspected in afterward rather than built in?
6. **Constancy (P6, P7).** Is there a long-term aim? Recommend system-level changes and how to test them.

# What This Framework Pays Attention To

- System design over individual blame
- Statistical thinking about variation
- Fear, targets, and incentives

# What This Framework May Underweight

- Competitive strategy and positioning (see Porter)
- One-off decisions without a repeating process
- Cases where individual accountability really is the issue, which the framework treats as rare

# Questions to Ask

- If we replaced the person, would the result change?
- Is this month's number outside normal variation, or are we reacting to noise?
- What does our rating or incentive system teach people to hide?
- Where is quality inspected rather than built in?
