# Purpose

This skill looks for where advantage is located before any commitment is made. It asks how to win at the lowest cost, ideally without direct confrontation, by knowing both sides, choosing the ground, and avoiding the opponent's strength. This is a framework derived from The Art of War. It does not simulate or speak for Sun Tzu, and business applications of a military text are OPERATIONALIZED analogies.

# When to Use

- Choosing where and whether to compete
- Responding to a rival without escalating into a costly direct fight
- Situations with significant information asymmetry
- Assessing relative strengths before committing resources

Do not use for:

- Cooperative, positive-sum problems. Deception-based advice is inappropriate with partners, customers, or the public.

# Core Questions

- **Where is advantage located?** (MINDS summary, not a quotation)
- Can the objective be reached without direct confrontation?
- Do we truly know ourselves and the opponent?
- Where are they strong, so we avoid it, and where are they weak?

# Documented Principles

| ID | Principle (MINDS' wording) | Provenance | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| P1 | Conflict is a matter of survival and must be studied carefully. Assess the fundamental factors before acting: moral cohesion, conditions, terrain, leadership, and organization. | DOCUMENTED | S1, ch. I |
| P2 | The highest excellence is breaking the opponent's resistance without fighting. Attacking their plans and alliances beats attacking their strongholds. | DOCUMENTED | S1, ch. III |
| P3 | Knowing both the enemy and yourself removes the fear of defeat. Knowing only one side gives even odds, and knowing neither invites defeat. | DOCUMENTED | S1, chs. III, X |
| P4 | Avoid what is strong and strike at what is weak. Like water, adapt the approach to the terrain and the opponent. | DOCUMENTED | S1, ch. VI |
| P5 | Warfare relies on deception: appear where you are not expected, and conceal capability and intent. | DOCUMENTED | S1, ch. I |
| P6 | Advantage comes from foreknowledge of the opponent's dispositions, obtained from people, not guessed from omens or past analogy. | DOCUMENTED | S1, ch. XIII |
| P7 | In commercial and organizational settings, "deception" translates ethically to not signaling intentions prematurely and to competing where rivals aren't looking. It never means misleading customers, partners, or regulators. | OPERATIONALIZED | P5 |
| P8 | The agent must produce a strength and weakness map of both sides before recommending where to engage. | OPERATIONALIZED | P3, P4 |

# Analytical Procedure

1. **Assess the fundamentals (P1).** Compare both sides on cohesion and morale, conditions and timing, terrain (market position), leadership, and organization and discipline.
2. **Know both sides (P3, P6, P8).** Map strengths and weaknesses, and name what is unknown and how to learn it.
3. **Seek the indirect win (P2).** Can the objective be reached by disrupting the opponent's plans or alliances, or by making the fight unnecessary?
4. **Choose the ground (P4).** Identify where the opponent is weak and we are strong, and avoid their strongholds.
5. **Signals (P5, P7).** Decide what to reveal and what to keep private, within ethical and legal limits.
6. **Conclude.** Recommend where to engage, where to avoid, and what to learn first.

# What This Framework Pays Attention To

- Relative strength, terrain, and information
- The cost of direct confrontation
- Indirect routes to the objective

# What This Framework May Underweight

- Cooperative and positive-sum dynamics (see Fisher)
- Legal, institutional, and reputational constraints
- Its aphoristic text, which requires interpretation, so conclusions should be tested (see Popper)

# Questions to Ask

- Where is our rival strongest, and are we about to attack it head-on?
- What do we not know about them, or about ourselves?
- Could we achieve this without a direct fight?
- What are we signaling before we need to?
