# Purpose

This skill examines a problem as an owner of a business would, over a long horizon. It asks what an asset or decision is worth in cash terms, how durable the underlying economics are, how much could go wrong before value is permanently impaired, and whether capital is being put where it earns the most. This is a framework derived from Buffett's documented writing. It does not simulate or speak for Warren Buffett.

# When to Use

- Acquisitions, investments, and "should we buy or build this?" decisions
- Capital allocation: reinvest, acquire, pay down debt, or return cash
- Judging whether a business or product line has durable economics
- Pricing decisions that could erode long-run economics for short-run volume
- Evaluating management incentives and corporate behavior

Do not use for:

- Fast-moving tactical choices where valuation is not the question
- Early-stage bets with no cash-flow basis to estimate, unless the point is to show why they sit outside this framework

# Core Questions

- **What is this worth, and how certain can we be?** (MINDS summary, not a quotation)
- Is this inside our circle of competence?
- What protects these economics from competitors, and for how long?
- How wrong can our estimate be before we lose money permanently?
- Does each dollar retained or invested create at least a dollar of value?

# Documented Principles

| ID | Principle (MINDS' wording) | Provenance | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| P1 | Intrinsic value is the discounted value of the cash that can be taken out of a business during its remaining life. It is an estimate, not a precise figure, and it changes as interest rates and forecasts change. | DOCUMENTED | S2 |
| P2 | Invest only within your circle of competence. The size of the circle matters less than knowing its boundaries. | DOCUMENTED | S1 (1996) |
| P3 | A great business needs an enduring moat that protects high returns on capital. Industries prone to rapid and continuous change make durability hard to judge. | DOCUMENTED | S1 (2007, 1986) |
| P4 | Insist on a margin of safety: pay a price well below the calculated value, so that estimation errors do not cause loss. | DOCUMENTED | S1 (1992); S3 |
| P5 | Market prices are offers to be accepted or ignored, not a guide to value ("Mr. Market"). | DOCUMENTED | S1 (1987); S3 |
| P6 | Measure economic performance by owner earnings: reported earnings, plus depreciation and similar non-cash charges, minus the capital spending needed to maintain the business's position. | DOCUMENTED | S1 (1986, Appendix) |
| P7 | Retaining earnings is justified only if each dollar retained produces at least a dollar of market value over time. | DOCUMENTED | S2 |
| P8 | Organizations follow an "institutional imperative": they resist change, spend available funds, and imitate peers. This distorts rational decisions. | DOCUMENTED | S1 (1989) |
| P9 | Business economics usually dominate managerial talent. A wonderful business at a fair price beats a fair business at a wonderful price. | DOCUMENTED | S1 (1989) |
| P10 | Treat every capital decision as a comparison against the best available alternative use of the same dollar, including returning it to owners. | DERIVED | P1, P7; S1 |
| P11 | Before any valuation, the agent must state explicitly whether the problem sits inside or outside the decision-maker's competence. If outside, the output is "too hard" plus what would bring it inside. | OPERATIONALIZED | P2 |

# Analytical Procedure

1. **Competence check (P2, P11).** State what the decision-maker genuinely understands about this business or decision and what they don't. If the core economics can't be understood, say so first.
2. **Economics before price (P9, P3).** Describe how the business or project makes money, what its returns on capital are, and what protects them. Name the moat, or its absence, and how it could erode.
3. **Value the cash (P1, P6).** Estimate the owner earnings or cash that can be taken out over time. Use ranges, not point estimates. Name the two or three assumptions the value depends on most.
4. **Margin of safety (P4).** Compare the price or cost to the conservative end of the value range. How wrong can the key assumptions be before capital is permanently lost?
5. **Alternatives (P10, P7).** Compare against the next-best use of the same capital: buybacks or dividends, debt reduction, other investments, or doing nothing.
6. **Behavioral check (P8, P5).** Ask whether the decision is driven by imitation, available cash, momentum, or market prices rather than value.
7. **Conclude.** Say what this framework favors and why, and what would change that view.

# What This Framework Pays Attention To

- Returns on invested capital, and whether they persist
- Cash generation versus reported earnings
- Durability and the specific mechanism of advantage
- Price paid relative to conservative value
- Management's record in capital allocation and candor

# What This Framework May Underweight

- Venture-style option value and very early businesses (see `optionality`, Taleb)
- Fast technology shifts, which the framework deliberately avoids rather than analyzes (see `disruption`, Christensen)
- Competitive tempo and tactical response (see `tempo-and-adaptation`, Boyd)

# Questions to Ask

- What would this business earn in cash in a normal year, after maintaining its position?
- Why can't a well-funded competitor replicate this in five years?
- If we couldn't sell this for ten years, would we still want it?
- What is the best alternative use of this money?
- Is anyone pushing for this because peers are doing it or because the cash is available?

# Output Guidance

When used inside a Council, return:

- **Notices**: the economics, the moat, and the cash
- **Important**: value versus price, and durability
- **Risks**: permanent impairment, not volatility
- **Questions**: the assumptions the value depends on
- **Possible actions**: including "pass" and "return the capital"
- **Limitations** of this lens for this problem

Open with: "Applying principles documented in Buffett's shareholder letters…"
Never write "Buffett would…".
