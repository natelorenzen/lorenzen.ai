# Purpose

This skill asks who is adapting faster, and why. It focuses on orientation, the mental models through which an organization interprets reality, and on whether the organization can act on that orientation quickly, with little explicit command. It also looks for ways to disorient a competitor. This is a framework derived from Boyd's briefings and papers. It does not simulate or speak for John Boyd, and commercial applications are OPERATIONALIZED.

# When to Use

- Fast-moving competition and responding to a rival's move
- Organizations that are slow because every action needs explicit approval
- When the organization's mental model of the market may be outdated
- Crisis response

Do not use for:

- Slow, long-horizon capital decisions where speed isn't the constraint

# Core Questions

- **Who is adapting faster?** (MINDS summary, not a quotation)
- Is our orientation, meaning our model of reality, current?
- Where is our cycle slow: observing, orienting, deciding, or acting?
- Could our people act rightly without waiting for explicit orders?

# Documented Principles

| ID | Principle (MINDS' wording) | Provenance | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| P1 | Observation, orientation, decision, and action form a set of continuous, interacting processes, not a simple sequential loop. | DOCUMENTED | S1; S3 |
| P2 | Orientation, shaped by culture, experience, analysis, and new information, is the most important element, because it shapes what is observed and what actions follow. | DOCUMENTED | S1; S3 |
| P3 | Operate inside the opponent's observation-orientation-decision-action time cycle, so their picture of reality falls behind events and they become confused and disoriented. | DOCUMENTED | S1; S3 |
| P4 | Well-trained organizations with shared orientation act through "implicit guidance and control", without explicit orders for each action, which makes them fast. | DOCUMENTED | S1; S3 |
| P5 | Mental models must be periodically destroyed and rebuilt. When a model's mismatch with reality grows, break it apart and synthesize a new one from the pieces. | DOCUMENTED | S2 |
| P6 | Tempo can be irregular. The aim is to act at a pace and pattern the opponent cannot read, not simply to cycle faster. | DERIVED | S3 |
| P7 | The agent must diagnose which part of the organization's cycle, whether observation, orientation, decision, or action, is the bottleneck. | OPERATIONALIZED | P1, P2 |

# Analytical Procedure

1. **Orientation audit (P2, P5).** State the organization's current mental model of the situation. Where is the mismatch with reality growing? Does the model need rebuilding, not just adjusting?
2. **Cycle bottleneck (P1, P7).** Where is time lost: information reaching decision-makers, interpretation, approval, or execution?
3. **Implicit control (P4).** Could teams act on shared intent without escalating? What shared understanding would they need?
4. **Opponent's cycle (P3, P6).** How fast is the rival's cycle, and what would make their picture of reality fall behind ours?
5. **Conclude.** Recommend what to change in orientation and cycle speed first.

# What This Framework Pays Attention To

- Speed and quality of adaptation
- Mental models and their decay
- Decentralized action through shared intent

# What This Framework May Underweight

- Valuation and capital decisions (see Buffett)
- Situations where patience beats tempo
- Its own over-simplification in popular form (the "OODA loop" diagram)

# Questions to Ask

- What do we believe about this market that was true three years ago but may not be now?
- How long does it take for a front-line observation to change what we do?
- What decisions do we escalate that a well-oriented team could just make?
- What would make our rival's model of us wrong?
