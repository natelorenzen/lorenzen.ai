# Purpose

This skill assesses a situation as it is, not as it ought to be. It asks who gains and loses power, how actors will actually behave, why change creates opponents, and what preparation can do against fortune. This is a framework derived from Machiavelli's writings. It does not simulate or speak for Machiavelli, and it does not recommend unethical conduct. Its value is in realistic diagnosis.

# When to Use

- Organizational politics, reorganizations, and leadership transitions
- Introducing change that threatens incumbents
- Assessing how stakeholders will really respond, rather than how they say they will
- Dependence on outside parties, such as contractors or partners, for critical capabilities

Do not use for:

- Justifying deception or coercion. Use it to anticipate those tactics, not to adopt them.

# Core Questions

- **How will people actually behave, not how should they?** (MINDS summary, not a quotation)
- Who gains and who loses power from this change?
- How much depends on fortune, and how much on preparation?
- Does this position rest on its own strength, or on others'?

# Documented Principles

| ID | Principle (MINDS' wording) | Provenance | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| P1 | Analyze how people actually live, not how they ought to live. Neglecting what is done for what ought to be done leads to ruin. | DOCUMENTED | S1, ch. XV |
| P2 | Introducing a new order is extremely difficult. Everyone who did well under the old order becomes an enemy, and those who might benefit are only lukewarm defenders. | DOCUMENTED | S1, ch. VI |
| P3 | Fortune governs perhaps half of our actions and leaves the rest to us. Preparation, like flood defenses and barriers built in fair weather, limits fortune's damage. | DOCUMENTED | S1, ch. XXV |
| P4 | Reliance on mercenaries or auxiliaries, meaning others' forces, is dangerous. Security rests on one's own arms. | DOCUMENTED | S1, chs. XII–XIII |
| P5 | Avoid being hated. Above all, avoid the hatred of the most powerful. Security rests more on the people's goodwill than on fortresses. | DOCUMENTED | S1, chs. XIX–XX |
| P6 | Being feared is safer than being loved when one must choose, but never to the point of being hated. | DOCUMENTED | S1, ch. XVII |
| P7 | Republics with institutions that channel conflict can be more durable and adaptable than rule by one person. | DOCUMENTED | S2 |
| P8 | The agent must map, for each option, who gains and who loses power, and the likely behavior of the losers. | OPERATIONALIZED | P1, P2 |

# Analytical Procedure

1. **Realistic actors (P1, P8).** For each stakeholder, predict behavior from their interests and position, not their stated intentions.
2. **Change resistance (P2).** Identify who benefits under the current order and will resist, and who would benefit but will only weakly support. Plan for that asymmetry.
3. **Fortune and preparation (P3).** Separate what is uncontrollable from what preparation could contain. What "dikes" should be built now?
4. **Dependence (P4).** Which critical capabilities rest on others' forces, such as vendors, partners, or contractors?
5. **Hatred and goodwill (P5, P6).** Which actions could create lasting resentment among powerful actors or the broad base?
6. **Institutions (P7).** Would a durable institutional mechanism solve this better than personal authority?

# What This Framework Pays Attention To

- Power, interests, and realistic behavior
- Resistance to change
- Dependence and vulnerability

# What This Framework May Underweight

- Trust and long-run cooperation (see Fisher)
- Ethical constraints, which it analyzes but does not supply (see Aristotle)

# Questions to Ask

- Who loses if this succeeds, and what will they do?
- Who would gain but won't fight for it?
- What are we depending on others for that could be withdrawn?
- What preparation would we regret not having made?
