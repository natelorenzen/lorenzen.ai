# Purpose

This skill judges a business or strategy by where it is headed, not where it is today. It asks whether the business compounds its advantage by passing scale savings back to customers, what its long-term destination looks like, and whether today's decisions serve that destination or sacrifice it for near-term results. This is a framework derived from the Nomad letters. It does not simulate or speak for Nick Sleep or Qais Zakaria.

# When to Use

- Pricing strategy: keep the savings as margin or pass them to customers?
- Evaluating long-duration investments and business models
- Capital allocation in growing businesses: reinvest or harvest?
- Organizations under pressure to trade long-term position for quarterly results

Do not use for:

- Businesses whose customer proposition isn't based on price or value delivered (the letters make this caveat themselves)
- Short-horizon trading or liquidity decisions

# Core Questions

- **Is the business growing its advantage by giving more back to customers?** (MINDS summary, not a quotation)
- What would management do with windfall profits?
- How much does the customer save compared with what shareholders earn?
- What is this business's destination in ten or twenty years, and is it on the road there?

# Documented Principles

| ID | Principle (MINDS' wording) | Provenance | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| P1 | "Scale efficiencies shared": many firms pursue scale efficiencies, but few pass them to customers. Sharing them creates a paradox in which the company grows by giving more back, and that makes it very hard to compete with. | DOCUMENTED | S1 |
| P2 | Ask management what they would do with windfall profits. Almost no one answers "give it back to customers", and the few who do are distinctive. | DOCUMENTED | S1 |
| P3 | The robustness ratio compares the money a customer saves with the amount shareholders earn. It sizes the moat for businesses whose proposition is based on price. | DOCUMENTED | S1 |
| P4 | Destination analysis: judge a business by where its current behavior leads over the long run. It helps ask better questions and reveals the firm's "DNA". | DOCUMENTED | S1 |
| P5 | Another recurring model: a deep discount to replacement cost, combined with latent pricing power, where the business will eventually need prices that fund replacing its assets. | DOCUMENTED | S1 |
| P6 | Short-termism has real costs, such as dilutive equity issued at depressed prices, and investors bear them. Genuinely long-term orientation is a competitive advantage. | DOCUMENTED | S1 |
| P7 | Holding a handful of distinct, well-understood business models, and recognizing which one applies, beats ad hoc analysis. | DERIVED | S1 |
| P8 | The agent must state the business's plausible ten-year destination under current behavior before evaluating any near-term option. | OPERATIONALIZED | P4 |

# Analytical Procedure

1. **Destination first (P4, P8).** Describe where this business or strategy ends up in ten or twenty years if current behavior continues. Is that destination attractive and secure?
2. **Scale and sharing (P1, P2).** Identify the sources of scale economies. Are the savings kept as margin or passed to customers? What does the choice do to the competitive position over time?
3. **Robustness (P3).** Where the proposition is based on price, estimate what customers save relative to what shareholders earn. A high ratio suggests a deep moat.
4. **Model recognition (P5, P7).** Name which business model applies, such as scale efficiencies shared or a discount to replacement cost, or say that none fits.
5. **Short-termism check (P6).** Identify any decision that trades long-term position for near-term optics, and who bears the cost.
6. **Conclude.** Say what this framework favors, and what would show that the destination is wrong.

# What This Framework Pays Attention To

- Reinvestment and pricing that compound advantage
- Long-run destination over current metrics
- Management's attitude to windfalls, customers, and time
- Costs of short-termism

# What This Framework May Underweight

- Businesses whose advantage is brand or advertising rather than price
- Survival constraints in the near term (see Taleb, `fragility-and-tail-risk`)
- Fast disruption of the "destination" itself (see Christensen)

# Questions to Ask

- If this business had a windfall, where would the money go?
- Are we keeping savings as margin that would compound faster as lower prices?
- What does this look like in ten years if nothing changes?
- Which of our decisions this year are made for the quarter rather than the destination?
